# Verifiers and lone skeptics outperform debate panels

**Yes, "round tables are not effective" still holds, and the 2025–2026 evidence makes it stronger.** The finding needs one refinement for your particular design. Every study that compares multi-agent debate against plain majority voting at matched compute finds that debate ties or loses while spending **2–3.4× the tokens**. That includes peer-reviewed work at ICLR 2024 and NeurIPS 2025 and two large 2026 preprints. A NeurIPS 2025 proof shows that vanilla debate cannot raise expected correctness: the gain comes from ensembling independent samples, not from agents talking to each other. Your Proponent/Skeptic/Arbiter panel is not classic consensus debate. The two advocates run in parallel and never see each other, so it avoids the worst documented failure, which is conformity collapse over several rounds. Its remaining parts are each weakly supported or contradicted by the evidence:

- Assigned personas do not improve accuracy.
- Votes locked to a role are not independent samples, so the quorum mostly reduces to the Arbiter's tie-break.
- LLM critics have a high base rate of nitpicks and false defects, and a 3/3 quorum turns that into escalations.
- The Arbiter inherits documented judge biases toward persuasion, verbosity, position and self-preference.

The evidence points to a different adjudicator for each decision type:

- **Completion verification:** an execution-grounded Verifier. This is the strongest finding in the whole corpus.
- **Design approval:** one fresh-context Skeptic whose objections must carry evidence and a severity, arbitrated item by item. For high-stakes cases, move up to N *independent* reviewers, ideally from different model families.
- **Triage:** the main model alone, with self-consistency voting for discrete calls.

Newer reasoning models make the case for agent interaction weaker, not stronger. The main caveat is that almost none of this evidence comes from open-ended design review, and no study tests quorum rules or your exact topology. So the design-approval recommendations are inference from neighbouring tasks, not direct measurement.

*Reading conventions.* Each source carries the tag its research note gave it:

- **[verified]**: the abstract, full text or venue page was read.
- **[snippet]**: known only from search-result text.
- **[memory]**: from prior knowledge of the literature and not re-checked. Treat specific numbers with this tag as approximate.

Sentences that begin with ***Inference:*** are this report's reasoning, not published results.

## Debate's gains are a voting effect bought at 2–3.4× the tokens

The 2023 papers behind multi-agent debate reported real gains, but almost all of them compared against a *single* sample or a self-reflection loop. Du et al. had several ChatGPT instances revise their answers over rounds ([arXiv 2305.14325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325), ICML 2024) [memory]. Liang et al. built an "angel/devil/judge" debate to escape "Degeneration-of-Thought," in which a confident model cannot produce new ideas through self-reflection ([arXiv 2305.19118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118), EMNLP 2024) [memory]. ChatEval used multi-agent debate as an evaluator ([arXiv 2308.07201](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07201), ICLR 2024) [verified]. These baselines mixed up two effects: having N samples at all, and letting those samples interact. The first matched-compute control already pulled them apart. Huang et al. re-ran Du's debate on GSM8K and counted responses. **Self-consistency with 6 responses scored 85.3% against debate's 83.2%, and with 9 responses 88.2% against 83.0%** ([ar5iv 2310.01798](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.01798), ICLR 2024) [verified]. The authors concluded that debate is better understood as a way to reach consistency across generations than as self-correction.

Everything since has confirmed this. Smit et al. found debate "does not reliably outperform" self-consistency and is "more sensitive to different hyperparameter settings" ([arXiv 2311.17371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371), ICML 2024) [snippet]. Wang et al. showed that a single agent with strong prompts nearly matches the best discussion method ([arXiv 2402.18272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18272), ACL 2024) [verified]. The decisive 2025 paper is Choi, Zhu and Li, "Debate or Vote" (NeurIPS 2025). It splits debate into a voting component and a communication component. On Qwen2.5-7B across seven benchmarks, **majority voting averaged 0.769 while the best debate variant reached 0.738, and accuracy *fell* as rounds increased (0.738 → 0.711 → 0.705 at T=2, 3 and 5)**. Their Theorem 2 proves that, when homogeneous agents update by averaging their neighbours' beliefs, belief in the correct answer is a martingale: debate leaves expected correctness unchanged ([arXiv 2508.17536](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2); [NeurIPS 2025](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/116557)) [verified]. A 2025 position paper testing 5 debate methods on 9 benchmarks says debate "often fail[s] to outperform" Chain-of-Thought and self-consistency "even when consuming significantly more inference-time computation" ([arXiv 2502.08788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)) [snippet].

The two largest 2026 studies agree, though both are preprints. *Beyond Symmetric Agents* ran **more than 5,500 debate and control runs across 23 open models from 11 vendor families**, and paired every configuration with a majority vote at the same generation budget. Debate beat a single agent by 3–7 points but **tied or lost to budget-matched self-consistency at 3.4× the tokens**. Persona prompting imposed a "persona tax," nearly all of debate's benefit arrived in the first exchange of answers, and the authors called the gain "an ensemble-sampling effect" ([arXiv 2609.35875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35875), Sep 2026) [verified abstract]. *The Cost of Consensus* found that isolated self-correction beat 10-agent, 3-round debate. On Ministral GSM-Hard the gap was **48.3% versus 20.7%**, and debate used 2.1–3.4× the tokens ([arXiv 2605.00914](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00914v1), Apr 2026) [verified].

Debate does win under some conditions. In each one, something correlated with correctness breaks the martingale:

- **Correctness-biased update rules.** A "conformist" update raised Choi's debate from 0.733 to 0.763, which is still at or below plain voting ([arXiv 2508.17536](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)) [verified].
- **Heterogeneous model families.** In one oracle analysis, cross-family pairs cut errors by more than 30% ([arXiv 2510.20963](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20963)) [verified]. The 2026 study above found the opposite: mixed-model teams lost to votes over their own rosters ([arXiv 2609.35875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35875)) [verified abstract].
- **Collaborative protocols designed by the authors.** ColMAD beat self-consistency at roughly matched tokens, 86.29 versus 81.98 F2 ([arXiv 2510.20963](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20963)) [verified]. Method papers evaluated by their own authors deserve a discount.

***Inference:*** for a quorum vote, the useful ingredient is the *independence and diversity of the first judgments*. Letting agents see each other mostly makes their errors correlated, which weakens the vote.

Reasoning models do not rescue debate. DeepSeek-R1 already gains a lot from plain voting: **79.8% → 86.7% on AIME 2024 with cons@64** ([arXiv 2501.12948](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948)) [snippet]. Qwen3-8B with thinking enabled changed its answers in debate far less than non-reasoning models, which the authors attribute to anchoring on long reasoning traces ([arXiv 2605.00914](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00914v1)) [snippet]. Pairing GPT-4o-mini with R1 in competitive debate collapsed accuracy to **24.89 F2** ("debate hacking"). In consensus debate, a strong agent can abandon a correct answer to reach early agreement with a weaker partner ([arXiv 2510.20963](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20963)) [verified]. Within a single trace, reasoning models' reflection is mostly confirmatory. Across 8 models it rarely changes the first answer, and cutting post-answer reflection saved 24.5% of tokens for a 2.9% accuracy loss ([arXiv 2510.08308](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08308)) [verified]. ***Inference:*** strong reasoners already debate internally, resist peer influence, and gain cheaply from voting. What other agents can still add is independent errors (a different model family) and new information (tools and evidence). Neither requires the agents to read each other.

## The panel is oversight debate, which pays only when debaters know more than the judge

Your design avoids most of the failure modes documented for debate, because those failures need agents to see each other over several rounds. In homogeneous debate, agents adopted the peers' modal answer **up to 85.5% of the time**, abandoned **up to 70% of correct answers** after seeing peers, and conformity appeared with as few as two peers ([arXiv 2605.00914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00914)) [verified]. Models "frequently shift from correct to incorrect answers in response to peer reasoning," even when stronger models outnumber weaker ones ([arXiv 2509.05396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05396), ICML 2025 workshop) [snippet]. Conformity is robust enough to have its own benchmark, BenchForm ([arXiv 2501.13381](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13381v2), ICLR 2025 oral) [verified]. ***Inference:*** running Proponent and Skeptic in parallel with fresh contexts and only one Arbiter read is the well-supported part of your design.

Structurally, a panel with assigned opposing sides and a judge who decides is *oversight debate*, not consensus debate. That literature is more favourable, but only under a specific condition. Khan et al. gave two debaters access to a story that the judge could not see. **Debate lifted non-expert judge accuracy to 76% for LLM judges and 88% for human judges, against 54% and 78% under consultancy (a single advocate)**. Making the debaters more persuasive *raised* judge accuracy, while making a lone consultant more persuasive *lowered* it ([arXiv 2402.06782](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06782v4), ICML 2024) [snippet]. DeepMind's broader replication found that **debate beats consultancy on every task, but results against direct judging were "mixed" on tasks without information asymmetry** ([arXiv 2407.04622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04622), NeurIPS 2024) [verified abstract]. ***Inference:*** your Arbiter reads the same draft and codebase as the advocates, so there is no information asymmetry, which is exactly Kenton et al.'s "mixed" regime. The one benefit that does hold, two opposed advocates beating one, is already present in alternative (1): the main model, holding the draft and its rationale, is the advocate, and the Skeptic is the opposition. The Proponent seat mostly restates the draft's case.

The vote count is weaker than it looks. ***Inference (untested):*** a Proponent briefed to support the draft and a Skeptic briefed to attack it do not cast independent samples of the verdict. Their votes are largely fixed by role. Under a 2/3 quorum the Arbiter is effectively the sole decider. Under 3/3, the Skeptic holds a veto. This is the reverse of the independence that makes majority voting work.

The Arbiter also carries every documented LLM-judge bias:

- **Persuasion.** Embedding persuasion techniques in *incorrect* math solutions raised their scores by up to about 8%. Larger models were not immune, and counter-prompting did not remove the effect ([arXiv 2508.07805](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2508.07805), EMNLP 2025 Findings) [verified].
- **Position.** In a study of 15 judges and over 150,000 instances, position bias was concentrated where solution quality was close, the "instances with disagreements" ([arXiv 2406.07791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07791)) [verified]. Those are exactly the cases a panel exists to decide.
- **Self-preference.** Judges favour their own generations, and the bias grows with self-recognition ([arXiv 2404.13076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076), NeurIPS 2024) [verified]. Self-preference tracks text familiarity (low perplexity), so it survives a fresh context ([arXiv 2410.21819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819)) [memory].
- **Bandwagon and authority.** Both persist in advanced judges ([arXiv 2410.02736](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736), ICLR 2025) [verified].
- **Peer sycophancy.** Identity-driven sycophancy toward peers is "far more common than self-bias," and anonymizing outputs reduces it without hurting accuracy much ([arXiv 2510.07517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07517v1)) [verified].

In software engineering specifically, R2E-Gym found that LLM patch verifiers are "biased and often rely on stylistic features." They attend most to the *agent's own thoughts* and appear to read confidence as a proxy for correctness ([arXiv 2504.07164](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07164), NeurIPS 2025) [verified]. ***Inference:*** a Proponent that argues the draft's case in fluent prose feeds the Arbiter exactly the signal that misleads LLM judges.

## Assigned skeptics plus unanimity manufacture escalations over nits

Labelling an agent "Skeptic" does not make it more accurate. Across 162 personas and 2,410 factual questions, adding a persona did not beat a no-persona control. Automatically picking the best persona was "often no better than random" ([arXiv 2311.10054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054), EMNLP 2024 Findings) [verified]. What helped in ChatEval was *diversity of perspectives*, such as general public, critic and psychologist. Giving every agent the same role "cannot effectively enhance the performance compared with single-agent setting" ([ICLR 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/25cc3adf8c85f7c70989cb8a97a691a7-Abstract-Conference.html)) [verified]. A 2025 study found that **balanced** agent roles beat both uniformly agreeable and uniformly contrarian panels ([arXiv 2509.23055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.23055)) [verified]. Liang's original debate paper also reported that *moderate* disagreement worked best ([arXiv 2305.19118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118)) [memory].

LLM critics produce false positives at a high base rate. In OpenAI's CriticGPT work, **"longer critiques are … more likely to include hallucinations and nitpicks"**, models that hallucinated more also caught more real bugs, and even after RL training the absolute nitpick rate stayed "quite high." Human-plus-critic teams hallucinated less than critics alone ([arXiv 2407.00215](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00215v1)) [verified]. Jin and Chen found that LLMs "frequently misclassify correct code implementations" as defective, a "bias towards over-correction." **Prompts that ask for explanations *and proposed corrections* raise the misjudgment rate** ([arXiv 2508.12358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12358), ASE 2025 NIER) [verified]. Their 2026 follow-up replicated this across five models and proposed a filter that accepts a critique only if the proposed fix changes test outcomes ([arXiv 2603.00539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00539)) [verified]. Anthropic's Claude Code guidance states the problem plainly: "A reviewer that's asked to find problems will always find *something*. If you fix every finding, you end up with extra abstraction layers and tests for cases that can't happen" ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

Design judgments are also the kind of question on which models cave most easily. Answer-flip rates under challenge were **20.9% on elementary math but 80.8% on moral disputes**. A counterargument attributed to the model's own earlier session raised flips by 7.1 points ([Who Flips?, 2606.16011](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.16011.md), 2026) [verified]. Models are "steadfastly overconfident" in their first answers yet "prone to excessive doubt when challenged" ([arXiv 2507.03120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03120v1)) [verified abstract].

***Inference:*** you observed panels requiring unanimity escalating over wording nits. The literature predicts that symptom. A role-forced critic with a high nitpick base rate, briefed to list issues and fixes, sitting in a quorum that does not separate blocking from non-blocking findings, will block on trivia. No published study tests 2/3 versus 3/3 thresholds for LLM panels, so this link rests on mechanism, not measurement.

## Execution evidence carries the gains in agentic software engineering

For completion verification the evidence is unambiguous. In Agentless's patch-selection ablation on SWE-bench Lite, **majority vote alone resolved 25.67%, adding regression tests raised that to 27.00%, and adding generated reproduction tests raised it to 32.00%**, at about $0.25 extra per issue ([arXiv 2407.01489](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01489v2)) [verified]. R2E-Gym compared verifiers on SWE-bench Verified. Execution-based verifiers plateaued near **43.7%** and execution-free (LLM) verifiers near **42.8%**, while a hybrid reached **51.0%** ([arXiv 2504.07164](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07164), NeurIPS 2025) [verified]. Pass@26 was 64.4%, so about 13 points of correct candidates went unselected, and up to 10% of generated tests were "toxic." Agent-written tests are a weak oracle unless they are independent of the author. An LLM judge's agreement with test outcomes was only **κ = 0.54** for GPT-4.1-mini ([arXiv 2602.11911](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11911v1), 2026) [verified]. An ICLR 2026 benchmark found LLM-judge verification strategies struggle on harder problems ([ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10012641)) [snippet].

Multi-agent software-engineering frameworks fare poorly once traces are analysed. The MAST study annotated **1,642 traces across 7 frameworks, found failure rates of 41–86.7%**, and noted that "MAS gains over single-agent frameworks or simple baselines like best-of-N sampling are often minimal." Explicit verifier roles in MetaGPT and ChatDev often performed "only superficial checks" such as whether the code compiles. The largest single intervention was a check against the **task objective, which raised success by 15.6%** ([arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3), NeurIPS 2025) [verified]. Where multi-agent coding papers do report gains, their ablations credit diversity or execution, not deliberation. In SWE-Debate, the largest ablation contributor (+10.0) was generating multiple fault-propagation chains, ahead of the debate plan (+6.0) ([arXiv 2507.23348](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.23348)) [snippet]. AgentCoder's useful separation is an *independent* test writer whose tests are executed ([arXiv 2312.13010](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.13010v3)) [verified, ablation numbers not retrieved].

Self-review alone, alternative (3), is the weakest option on judgment tasks. With no external signal, self-correction did not improve reasoning and often turned correct answers wrong ([arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798), ICLR 2024) [memory for per-dataset numbers]. A TACL survey found no successful self-correction from prompted LLM feedback "except for studies in tasks that are exceptionally suited for self-correction" ([arXiv 2406.01297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)) [verified]. GPT-4 self-critique on planning produced a "significant performance collapse," while a sound external verifier produced "significant performance gains." Simply re-prompting with the verifier's verdict captured most of the benefit ([arXiv 2402.08115](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08115), ICLR 2025) [verified].

Fresh context still helps even with the same model. Models fixed errors presented as external but missed the identical errors in their own output, a **64.5% average "blind spot"** across 14 non-reasoning models ([arXiv 2507.02778](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778), NeurIPS 2025) [verified]. Reasoning models critiqued their own long chain-of-thought worse than others'. DeepSeek-R1 scored about 36% lower on its own outputs, and the best critic F1 overall was only about 41 ([arXiv 2502.19361](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.19361)) [verified].

Practice points the same way. A two-stage detect-then-filter reviewer reached **75% precision** at ByteDance ([arXiv 2501.15134](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15134v1)) [verified]. A deployment across 238 practitioners saw **73.8%** of automated comments resolved, but PR closure time *rose* from 5h52m to 8h20m ([arXiv 2412.18531](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18531), ICSE 2025 SEIP) [verified]. Cognition reports that its review loop "work[s] best when the coding and review agents do not share any context beforehand." The reviewer finds about 2 bugs per PR, about 58% of them severe, and the coding agent filters findings against the user's context. Cognition calls swarms "mostly a distraction" ([Cognition, Apr 2026](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)) [blog]. Anthropic finds that multi-agent systems "work mainly because they help spend enough tokens," and that "most coding tasks" are a poor fit ([Anthropic, Jun 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)) [blog]. Its Claude Code docs add that with the same model a subagent gives "parallelism or a fresh pair of eyes … but not a smarter answer" ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

## Match the adjudicator to the decision type

| Decision type | Best-supported adjudicator | Evidence strength | Deciding evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Completion verification** | (4) Verifier that independently reproduces each success criterion by executing it; optional LLM reviewer as tie-breaker for things tests don't cover | **Strong, direct** (SWE-bench-style, peer-reviewed) | Reproduction tests carried Agentless's largest gain; hybrid execution-plus-LLM verification beat either alone (R2E-Gym); a task-objective check was MAST's biggest fix; LLM-judge κ≈0.54 against tests |
| **Design approval / scope choice** | (1) Single fresh-context Skeptic, with severity-tagged, evidence-backed objections arbitrated item by item by the main model. For high blast radius, move up to (2): N independent reviewers with different perspectives or model families, aggregated by "any evidenced blocking finding" | **Weak, indirect**: no study of panels on design review | Fresh context unlocks error-finding (blind spot, DeltaBench); critics plus a filter beat critics alone (CriticGPT, BitsAI-CR); independence beats interaction (Debate or Vote); role personas add nothing |
| **Triage** (classify, prioritize, route, accept/reject findings) | (3) Main model alone for low stakes; self-consistency over k samples for discrete labels, escalating when the samples disagree | **Moderate** for discrete choices (voting literature), weak for self-review | Majority voting is the robust winner on short-answer decisions; intrinsic self-review alone does not improve judgments |

***Inference:*** the evidence supports keeping a panel *tier* but changing what it is made of. For design approval, the strongest-supported upgrade path has three steps. Start with one fresh Skeptic. Move up to two or three *unassigned* independent reviewers, each given a different checklist (correctness, constraints and knowledge conflicts, blast radius) rather than a stance. Escalate to a human when reviewers disagree on a *blocking* item. That path matches the independence of majority voting, ChatEval's perspective diversity and the oversight literature's use of two sides, without paying for a Proponent seat.

If you keep the Proponent/Skeptic/Arbiter shape, the evidence suggests these fixes. All of them are inferences, and none has been tested as a package:

- **The Arbiter commits a verdict before reading the reviews**, then changes it only by citing a new verifiable fact. This draws on the conformity results.
- **Present the reviews anonymized as "Review A/B" in randomized order**, with no stance labels ([arXiv 2510.07517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07517v1); [arXiv 2406.07791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07791)).
- **Count only blocking findings against the quorum.** Fold non-blocking suggestions into "accept-with-fixes," and default to 2/3, never 3/3 with a structurally negative seat.
- **Every blocking objection must name a concrete consequence and, where possible, a check that tells the original from the fix** ([arXiv 2603.00539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00539)).
- **Brief the Skeptic by task, not by attitude.** For example: "find defects that make this decision wrong; reporting none is valid". Do not demand a fix for every point ([arXiv 2508.12358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12358)).
- **Give reviewers the artifact, not the author's narrative** ([arXiv 2504.07164](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07164)), and use a different model family for a seat where feasible.

## Where the evidence runs thin

Four gaps limit how far these conclusions transfer.

**Task mismatch.** Nearly every matched-compute result comes from short-answer benchmarks with verifiable answers, often run on 7–32B open models. Choi et al. note that majority vote cannot even be applied to free text ([arXiv 2508.17536](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)). The only design-document studies are small and do not compare reviewer structures ([arXiv 2509.09975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09975v1); [arXiv 2601.19693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19693v1)). Design approval is therefore exactly where the evidence is weakest and the recommendation rests most on inference.

**Untested topology.** No study tests your exact topology: parallel assigned-stance advocates, a separate judge who also votes, and a quorum. No study tests quorum thresholds or false-escalation rates. The martingale theorem assumes symmetric neighbour-averaging, not assigned stances.

**Frontier models.** No matched-compute debate-versus-voting study uses o3, Claude extended thinking or GPT-5-class models. The reasoning-model evidence relies on R1, R1-distills and Qwen3, often in appendices. The R1-distill rows in the 2026 study are [snippet] only.

**Contested points.** Whether model heterogeneity helps *debate* more than it helps *voting* is contested ([arXiv 2510.20963](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20963) versus [arXiv 2609.35875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35875)). The positive debate results that do exist (ColMAD, Free-MAD, SDRL) are author-evaluated methods. Several foundational numbers are [memory]: Du et al.'s gains, ChatEval's exact table, the MT-Bench position-consistency figures and FlipFlop's flip rates. The practitioner guidance from Anthropic and Cognition is experience-based, with no controlled single-versus-panel comparison.

## Conclusion

Across three years of evidence, three ingredients account for what works in multi-agent review: **independence** (judgments formed before exposure to peers), **diversity** (different perspectives, checklists or model families) and **grounding** (execution, tests, or citations to the codebase and knowledge base). None of the three needs agents to argue, and none needs them to wear stance personas. The round table bundles one of these, fresh-context parallelism, with two things the evidence says are inert or harmful: role-locked votes and a judge exposed to persuasion. That is why it costs more without measurably helping. The practical reframing is to stop counting *agreement* and start counting *evidence*. "Any blocking finding that survives a check escalates" is a rule the literature supports. "Three seats must concur" is not.

Because the field has almost no data on design review, the most valuable next step is local. Log every panel and reviewer escalation, along with whether the human ultimately sided with the blocking finding. A few dozen of those outcomes would tell you more about your design-approval tier than any of the published benchmarks can.
